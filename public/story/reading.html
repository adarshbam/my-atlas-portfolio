A personal archive · 2016–2026

Still curious.

A brick, a pebble, and a question from my brother. That is where this story really begins.

Start at the beginning

01Childhood

The notebooks I never finished.

I was a quiet kid at school. If I did not understand something, I usually kept the question to myself. Asking a teacher felt difficult, and homework rarely held my attention long enough for me to finish it.

A class might finish twelve chapters while my notebook stopped at three. That was the kind of gap I remember. My handwriting was untidy, my notebooks were incomplete, and memorising things because I was told to felt tedious. Even so, I usually finished around third in my class, sometimes fourth or fifth.

School saw one version of me. Around people I knew, I had much more to say. There were questions everywhere, and my older brother always seemed willing to explore them with me.

A page I kept

This notebook page, from around 2016, belongs to the school years I described. Neat handwriting rarely held my attention.

02Childhood

My brother kept my curiosity alive.

Once, he asked which would hit the ground first: a large brick or a small pebble. I was sure the brick would win. Then he dropped them together. They landed together, and the answer I had been so certain of suddenly made no sense.

He did this kind of thing often. A question about jumping inside a moving train became a demonstration with a stone and a moving hand. He helped me see how something could keep moving even after it left your hand. Everyday objects became ways to understand the world.

Soon I wanted to know about gravity, stars, black holes, time travel and parallel universes. In second grade, I was reading science books meant for much older students. I enjoyed trying to work out their maths problems, too—looking at a triangle and finding a way through it before I knew the formal method.

In a group my brother taught, I learned multiplication tables up to twenty while older students were practising alongside me. I knew everyone there. I felt comfortable enough to try.

That is the part I want to remember most: my brother kept the curiosity of a little kid alive.

Having someone to show

My brother stayed part of the things I learned. Later, when I began programming, I would show him what I had made and explain it.

03Before 2017 → January 2017

I wanted to understand Ben 10.

Before I wrote any code, I was fascinated by phones. My father’s keypad phone could play films. Another phone had Kung Fu Panda. A tiny device could hold these whole worlds—and somehow let us speak to someone far away.

My brother used to download Doraemon episodes over a painfully slow connection. I remember him finding a particular spot with reception and waiting for a small, low-resolution episode to arrive. I would watch what he had managed to download.

During those childhood years, I lost my father.

Later, on an Intex phone, I began watching Ben 10. A friend had told me about the aliens, and I wanted to see everything. But many of the episodes I wanted were only available in English.

At the same time, my brother was learning English through A. J. Hoge’s lessons about listening and imitation. I made the connection: watching Ben 10 could also be my English practice. I listened, repeated the sounds, and recorded myself speaking.

Those recordings still exist. In January 2017, I was practising English on camera and making my own Ben 10 watch. Something I wanted to enjoy had given me a reason to learn.

04July 2017

Three hours. Two numbers.

My brother bought a laptop for his studies. His friend Faz was doing programming, and soon we were trying it too.

Our first program added two numbers. Faz helped over a voice call while we typed each piece ourselves. A missing semicolon, a bracket in the wrong place, another error. We did not have a fast video connection to make any of it easier.

It took about three hours. The photograph still shows that little program on a blue editor screen.

My brother and I started learning C together. After a while he stopped, but I kept going. I wanted to know what else I could make the computer do.

06September–November 2017

I made the car move.

I followed programming tutorials and worked through loops, operators and patterns. Then I discovered that code could draw. I could put lines on a screen, build a shape from them, and make that shape move.

I wanted to make games. My moving car was a long way from the one I had imagined, but it moved. I drew other cars, an aircraft and a character, and tried to turn sound functions into a simple piano. When I made something I was pleased with, I showed my brother and explained how it worked.

There were plenty of frustrating days too. Moving from Turbo C to Code::Blocks felt like an upgrade, until I spent a whole day trying to run a simple program.

Faz had also suggested Blender. I tried it in September and found it complicated. Toward the end of the year, watching MJO made me want to create animated videos myself, so I returned to 3D. By November I was also writing HTML in Notepad. Several different paths were opening at once.

08September 2017 → March 2018

I wanted to make the videos, too.

Blender began as another suggestion from Faz. I tried it in September 2017 and found it complicated. Watching MJO later gave me a reason to return to it.

I wanted to make videos like the ones I enjoyed watching. By January 2018, I was building an MJO-inspired scene; in March I was working on a Technical Guruji rap-video scene.

I also taught programming and animation on my own YouTube channel. Its name was a long one: Animation / Programming Guru. Learning something and wanting to show someone else were already closely connected for me.

10Early 2018

Online, I was “Beginner”.

In January, I joined SoloLearn. Coding on a phone felt surprisingly social: lessons, challenges, levels, people to compete with. I wanted to learn everything.

I practised HTML, CSS and C++, then moved into more languages as I worked through the challenges. One saved screenshot shows my profile at number one in India for that month.

I also remember hearing about someone reaching Platinum in three days. I made a new account called Beginner and tried to beat that time. As I remember it, I reached Platinum in a day and a half—and the account was banned because my progress was suspected of being cheating.

People in that community came to know me as Beginner. I was still the shy child at school, but here I could spend hours learning, competing and talking about what I was making.

11January–February 2018

A to Z in 1.510 seconds.

Alongside coding, I practised typing with all ten fingers. With enough focus, I could get through some exercises at around a hundred words per minute. One January screenshot shows 104.

Then I found a site where the whole challenge was typing the alphabet. I practised A to Z again and again until my best time reached 1.510 seconds.

I remember being second on the world leaderboard that day. The result screenshot survived; I had named it with the date and how that moment felt.

It was a very particular skill, earned through a lot of repetition. At the time, seeing my practice turn into a result I could measure was an incredible feeling.

12February–April 2018

Even centring a loader was a story.

CSS gave me another way to make things I could see. I followed tutorials, built a phone in the browser, experimented with logos and shapes, and shared small projects on SoloLearn.

The archive has several screenshots of one rainbow loader. In some, the word “Loading” sits awkwardly at the edge. I kept changing it. Eventually I saved a version with a very specific filename: “Finally center loading with rainbow”.

Other experiments were smaller: a first HTML form, a Google logo, a DNA-like pattern in the coding playground, a post celebrating a hundred followers. By April, I was looking for a domain where I could put my simple websites.

I was learning by making something, getting stuck, and trying another version.

13The next experiments · dates approximate

Games I could actually play.

Wanting to make games eventually brought me to Unity and C#. The programming and 3D work I had already done started to connect.

I made a Flappy Bird game and played it on my own phone. I also built a small first-person game with a boar chasing the player through a forest. You had to keep moving and shoot before it reached you. I was learning controls, collisions, shooting and game physics by putting them together.

Then I tried something much harder: a racing game with cars that would learn to drive and race alongside a human player. I could follow parts of how training worked, but the mathematics was beyond what I knew. I did not finish that game.

The smaller games are no longer in my archive. I still remember the feeling of playing something I had built, and how much the unfinished racing project taught me.

14Around October 2020

A bus became my first paid project.

I had seen people find paid 3D work online. I asked my brother to help me try. He created an Upwork account, and we searched and applied together.

Our first client needed a model for a bus conversion. I remember starting around October 2020 and spending roughly two months on it. The first payment was $100.

The client liked the work and came back for another addition: a cabin on top of the bus. In total, we earned $180 from that client.

Further work proved difficult to find. I had little professional experience, and my laptop limited the size of projects I could take on. After more applications without another client, I stepped away from 3D freelancing.

The tour of that bus still exists. It records a project I worked through for someone else, delivered, and was paid for.

15Late 2020 onward

I chose to focus on the web.

Around this period, Faz told us about a website commission that had earned him $500. It encouraged me to keep learning. After a pause, I decided to focus on web development properly.

I returned to HTML and CSS, worked on responsive layouts, followed courses and practised with Frontend Mentor challenges. School exams interrupted the rhythm sometimes, but I kept coming back. This time I wanted a set of skills I could use together.

Around the turn of 2021–2022, I did paid web work for the founder of EazyGrad, making pages responsive with HTML and CSS. It was my first paid web project. I continued into JavaScript and React from there.

16Harigurus → ProCodrr

Building for people. Helping people build.

At Harigurus, I worked on a service that helped people book purohits for weddings, mundan and other ceremonies. The work now involved real people trying to get something done.

When operations paused, I moved into social media management and helping develop courses for Anurag Singh’s ProCodrr channel. I spent about three years working on that side of learning: supporting courses, building projects and helping with questions.

I think about the child who could not ask a teacher when something was unclear. Helping someone else work through a doubt feels especially meaningful from here.

17October 2026

From Turbo C to Octoverse.

Finding my old GitHub profile brought another part of the journey back into view. The experiments had left traces in more places than the photographs.

On 24 October 2026, I bought a Claude subscription and began working on Octoverse, my more complex universe project. My tools now include Claude Opus 5.5, ChatGPT Astra and Gemini Argon.

Looking from those early Turbo C and Notepad screens to the tools I use now makes the distance feel real. I am still building, still learning what the tools can do, and still finding things I want to understand.

My brother kept my curiosity alive. I am still following it.
